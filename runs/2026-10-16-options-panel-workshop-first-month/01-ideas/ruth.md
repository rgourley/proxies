# Ruth, Financial educator, runs a free investing course for adults

## Idea 1: Run every first trade twice, once on paper

**Idea**: Before a learner places a real options trade, they place the same trade in a practice account with fake money. The app then replays it day by day until it expires. For example: you buy one call option on a $50 stock for $1.20 a share. That is $120 in total. It gives you the right to buy at $55 within three weeks. The replay shows that option worth $0.60 after ten quiet days, even though the stock did not fall.

**Why it matters to me**: People learn the second time they see something, not the first. In my classes, adults only grasp how an option loses value over time after they watch their own number shrink. A definition does not do it. I would rather they see that $60 vanish in practice than in their real account.

**What it assumes**: The practice account behaves like the real one. That means the same prices, the same expiry dates, and the same assignment rules.

## Idea 2: A "what happens Friday" card on every open position

**Idea**: Each open option shows a short, plain card. It says what happens on expiry day if the stock ends above, at, or below the agreed price, in dollars, for this exact position. For example: "If the stock closes at $56, you will be assigned. You must buy 100 shares for $5,500."

**Why it matters to me**: Expiry and assignment are where my students panic and call for help. They do not lack the definition. They have never seen it applied to their own numbers.

**What it assumes**: The app can compute these outcomes correctly for every position. A wrong number on this card would be worse than no card.

## Idea 3: A weekly "check yourself" question, with no penalty

**Idea**: Once a week, the app asks one question about the learner's own recent trade. For example: "Your option lost 30% while the stock was flat. Why?" A wrong answer leads to a worked example, not a warning.

**Why it matters to me**: Learners need a safe way to find out they misunderstood something before it costs them. Shame makes adults stop asking. I see this every term.

**What it assumes**: Learners will answer a question they could skip. That only holds if it is short and never blocks them from trading.
