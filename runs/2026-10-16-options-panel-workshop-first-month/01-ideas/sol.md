# Sol, Compliance officer at a retail brokerage

## 1. A first-month approval level that grows with proven understanding

**Idea:** Every new options account starts at the lowest approval level for its first month. That level allows only covered calls, cash-secured puts, and buying calls or puts with a fixed maximum loss. The customer moves up a level only after they pass a short knowledge check on expiry, assignment, and time decay, and the firm reviews them again.

**Why it matters to me:** The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) Rule 2360 requires us to do due diligence before we approve an options account and to approve each customer only for strategies that fit them. Right now most firms ask about "experience" and the customer ticks a box. A recorded knowledge check gives us real evidence for the approval. If a regulator asks why we approved this customer for spreads in week three, I can show them the answer.

**What it assumes:** The firm accepts slower upgrades and fewer options trades in month one.

## 2. Risk notices inside the order ticket, timed to the risk

**Idea:** Put short, plain-language risk notices on the order screen at the moment they apply. Examples: "This option expires Friday. If it ends in the money, you may be assigned 100 shares." Or: "You are selling an option. Your possible loss is larger than the premium you collect." The customer must see the notice before they can submit. We save a record of each notice they saw and when.

**Why it matters to me:** We must deliver the Options Disclosure Document, the industry's standard booklet on options risk, before approval. Almost nobody reads all of it. A risk notice that a customer actually sees beats a link at the bottom of the page. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) Rule 17a-4 also means I need to reproduce what the customer saw on that date.

**What it assumes:** We keep the notices short and specific so that customers do not learn to click past them.

## 3. Lessons and practice trades that never point at a real trade

**Idea:** Teach the first month with a practice account that uses no real money, and with worked examples on made-up tickers and made-up prices. No lesson links straight to a live order ticket. No lesson names a real stock.

**Why it matters to me:** Education is fine. A recommendation dressed as education is a problem. If a lesson says "here is how a put on a real stock works this week" and gives a "trade it" button, that can read as a recommendation. A recommendation brings in Regulation Best Interest, the SEC rule that requires us to act in the customer's best interest when we recommend something. FINRA Rule 2210 on communications with the public also applies to lesson content.

**What it assumes:** Made-up examples still teach well enough that customers carry the lesson into real trades.
